# Reflection orders, dihedral combinatorics, and paths

Root geometry supplies an induced dihedral Coxeter system inside each root plane. This topic uses those systems to describe relative reflection graphs, recover the full reflection-edge relation from order data, and interpret ordered edge products as path polynomials.

The inputs are positive roots, their reflection correspondence, and ambient inversion counts. The outputs are the relative dihedral structures, edge reconstruction and transport, and the path identities and plane counts used by the later sheaf comparison.

## Geometric orders on positive roots

Choose a linear form \(h\) positive on every simple root. Since a positive root is a nonnegative, nonzero combination of simple roots, \(h(\alpha)>0\) for every positive root \(\alpha\). Choose another functional \(f\) for which the ratios
\[
s(\alpha)=\frac{f(\alpha)}{h(\alpha)}
\]
are all distinct.

This assigns each positive root a real score. Equivalently, divide each root by \(h(\alpha)\), placing it on the affine hyperplane \(h=1\), and then evaluate \(f\).

For two independent roots in a plane, their normalized points and all other normalized roots in that plane lie on an affine line. A linear functional orders points on a line in one of its two directions. This explains the rank-two angular-order condition behind a geometric reflection order.

The generic-functional theorem proves that \(f\) can be chosen to separate the whole root set in finite generator rank. For a fixed interval only finitely many edges need to be listed, even if the group has infinitely many positive roots.

## Angular endpoints from finite inversion sets

Fix a root plane \(P\). The induced subgroup will be generated by its two angular endpoint reflections. To construct the endpoints, begin with the finite set of roots in \(P\) inverted by one chosen reflection.

Take its minimum and maximum slopes. If neither were an extreme of the entire plane, there would be positive roots outside both ends. The root defining the chosen reflection would lie in their positive cone. Both outside roots would remain positive under that reflection, forcing their positive combination to remain nonnegative, although the defining root is sent to its negative. This contradiction gives a global extreme.

An extreme root inverts only itself among the positive roots of the plane. Pairing it with another root of nonpositive Tits pairing and repeating the finite-inversion argument produces the opposite extreme. Thus every root plane has a plane basis.

## Generation measured by the plane inversion count

Define
\[
L_P(w)=\#\{a\in\Phi^+\cap P:w^{-1}a\text{ is negative}\}.
\]
This count is finite because it selects a subset of the ambient inversions. A plane-simple reflection changes it by \(+1\) or \(-1\), according to the sign of its root. The plane-weight identity proves this by making the extra plane contribution zero.

Every plane reflection inverts at least one endpoint: otherwise it would preserve positivity of the entire cone between them, including its own root. For a reflection different from that endpoint reflection, conjugating by the endpoint lowers \(L_P\) by two.

Strong induction on this nonnegative count proves that the endpoint reflections generate every reflection in \(P\), hence all of \(D_P\). The subgroup generated by two distinct involutions has the dihedral Coxeter presentation with exponent \(\operatorname{ord}(st)\), including the infinite-order case. This constructs the induced system on the actual subgroup.

## Signed roots identify the intrinsic inversions

The induced signed relation records three pieces of data: an ambient root representing a subgroup element, membership in \(P\), and its positive or negative sign. A simple endpoint reflection changes the sign exactly at its own positive root, matching the signed Coxeter action.

Applying this compatibility to a reduced induced word identifies negative ambient root images with its intrinsic inversion sequence. It follows that intrinsic reflections are exactly the ambient reflections belonging to \(D_P\), and
\[
\ell_P(w)=L_P(w).
\]
Thus the group presentation and the ambient root count describe the same length.

## Least coset representatives and relative intervals

Choose \(a_0\) of least ambient length in \(D_Pa\). Its inverse preserves every positive plane root: a negative image would make the corresponding plane reflection lower its length. It preserves negative roots by linearity as well.

Consequently \(w^{-1}r\) and \((wa_0)^{-1}r\) have the same sign for every plane root \(r\). This identifies the induced reflection graph with the ambient reflection graph restricted to the coset, preserving left labels, and gives
\[
L_P(wa_0)=\ell_P(w).
\]
The count also proves uniqueness of \(a_0\): another minimum has zero plane inversions, so its relative group factor has length zero.

Relative Bruhat order is reachability by these edges inside the coset. Its intervals map monotonically and injectively into ambient intervals. In type \(A_3\), the plane generated by \(\alpha_1+\alpha_2\) and \(\alpha_3\) has an induced simple reflection of ambient length three. Its two-vertex relative interval sits inside a larger ambient interval containing vertices outside the subgroup.

## Reflections and odd length gaps

In a two-generator Coxeter system, every odd alternating word is a reflection: the length-one case is simple, and a longer odd alternating word is a conjugate of a shorter odd alternating word by its outer simple generator. Every element has a reduced alternating expression. Together with parity of reflection length, this gives
\[
w\text{ a reflection}\Longleftrightarrow\ell(w)\text{ odd}.
\]

Since \(\ell(yx^{-1})\equiv\ell(y)+\ell(x)\pmod2\), a directed reflection edge is exactly
\[
\ell(x)<\ell(y),\qquad \ell(y)-\ell(x)\text{ odd}.
\]
The [[node:kl.dihedral.odd_reflection_edges|odd-gap edge theorem]] records both directions. It also makes the product of two elements of opposite parity a reflection after inversion of either one.

For interpolation, an opposite-parity external vertex \(y\le c\), \(y\not\le x\), has length greater than \(x\): a shorter vertex would lie below \(x\) by the dihedral Bruhat comparison. Thus these vertices are precisely the supported outgoing neighbors of \(x\).

## Finite subsets inside a principal dihedral interval

To transport a model from an interval to a finite relative lower ideal \(K\), choose a common upper bound for its vertices. If dihedral lengths are bounded, an element of maximal length has both simple generators as descents; induction on generator words shows that every element lies below it. If lengths are unbounded, choose an element longer than every vertex of \(K\), and use the dihedral comparison of strictly different lengths.

The [[node:kl.dihedral.finite_common_upper|finite-common-upper-bound theorem]] therefore places \(K\) inside an intrinsic interval \([1,d_0]_D\). The inclusion preserves and reflects the intrinsic order. This gives a finite interval model whose supported edges can be pulled back to the coset graph.

## Edge-transfer matrices and path composition

For an edge \(x\to y\), let \(E_{xy}\) be the matrix with a single \(1\) in row \(x\), column \(y\). On a column vector it copies coordinate \(y\) into coordinate \(x\). Define
\[
M_{xy}=I+T E_{xy}.
\]
It leaves every coordinate unchanged except that \(T\) times the upper coordinate is added to the lower one.

On a three-vertex chain \(x\to y\to z\), expand
\[
\begin{aligned}
M_{xy}M_{yz}
&=(I+TE_{xy})(I+TE_{yz})\\
&=I+TE_{xy}+TE_{yz}+T^2E_{xz}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The last term appears because \(E_{xy}E_{yz}=E_{xz}\): the output at \(y\) of the second matrix is read by the first.

Reversing the factors gives no \(T^2E_{xz}\), since \(E_{yz}E_{xy}=0\). Thus order matters. In a general product, choose either \(I\) or the edge term from each factor. A product of edge terms survives exactly when those edges form a composable path in the written order. Its contribution is \(T^{\text{number of edges}}\).

The rightmost matrix acts first. Therefore chronological application and the written matrix-product order are reversed. This is why the code distinguishes the chronological schedule from the decreasing path order.

## An A2 path calculation

Return to \(W=\langle s,t\rangle\) with \(sts=tst=w_0\). Put \(r=sts\), the third reflection, and take the reflection order
\[
s<r<t.
\]
For decreasing labels, the paths from \(1\) to \(w_0\) are:

| Path | Left-reflection labels | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| \(1\to w_0\) | \(r\) | \(T\) |
| \(1\to t\to ts\to w_0\) | \(t,r,s\) | \(T^3\) |

For example, \(rt=ts\) and \(s(ts)=w_0\), which checks the labels in the second row. The other saturated chains have labels \(s,r,t\) along \(1,s,st,w_0\), labels \(s,t,s\) along \(1,s,ts,w_0\), and labels \(t,s,t\) along \(1,t,st,w_0\). None is decreasing. Every edge increases length by at least one, and every reflection reverses length parity. A path from length zero to length three therefore has one or three edges, so the two displayed paths exhaust the possibilities.

The decreasing path sum is \(T+T^3\). The geometric Dyer theorem identifies this path construction with the already-defined normalized R-family, giving \(\widetilde R_{1,w_0}(T)=T+T^3\).

The direct edge jumps three ranks but contributes \(T\), because path weight counts edges, not rank difference. This is a useful distinction when reading the general path formula.

## Identification of the path polynomial with the R-polynomial

The matrix expansion gives an ordered path sum. To identify it with the Hecke bar coefficient, the proof establishes the same recursion and then compares geometric orders.

The formal proof first constructs an adapted order for which the path polynomials satisfy the same descent recursion as the normalized R-family. Equal diagonal values, zero values outside the order, and that recursion identify the two constructions.

It then compares geometric orders. When a generic functional is varied, an ordering change can be isolated to a complete root-plane block. On a relative dihedral interval, reversing that block preserves the relevant path entry through a path involution. Joining functionals through suitable transverse choices therefore preserves the full entry.

The Dyer entry develops these recursion and order-comparison steps. The OpenAI notes cite a general reflection-order result; the Lean development proves the geometric-order version used here.

## Reflection-edge reconstruction and transported schedules

An interval order isomorphism preserves covers directly. Reflection-edge reconstruction extends this transport to longer edges, such as \(1\to w_0\).

Diamond closure starts from covers and repeatedly applies rank-two configurations visible in the order. One theorem shows that the procedure adds only genuine reflection edges; the other shows that it obtains every reflection edge. Together they identify the recovered relation with the actual reflection graph.

Now an isomorphism \(\phi\) transports each source reflection edge to a target reflection edge. We can assign it the score of that target edge and list the source edges in that order. This is the transported schedule.

The scores come from the target realization. Relative-dihedral transport identifies the relevant rank-two configurations, and its counting theorems provide the schedule properties used in the source comparison.

## Edge factors and their degrees

Fix a source edge \(e:x\to y\) with root label \(\alpha_e\). Work in the edge coefficient ring \(A/(\alpha_e)\). Other root labels are reduced modulo \(\alpha_e\). The edge factor \(f_e\) is a product of these reduced linear factors with multiplicities determined by root-plane counts.

The nonzero reduced linear factors make this product nonzero in the quotient domain. The geometric plane counts determine its homogeneous degree:
\[
\deg f_e=\frac{\ell(y)-\ell(x)-1}{2}.
\]
For a cover this is zero. For the long edge \(1\to w_0\) in the example it is one, so the correction has degree one.

The [[node:kl.local.factor_relative_degree|relative edge-degree theorem]] identifies each individual weight with the intrinsic dihedral formula on the least-representative coset:
\[
2k_P(za_0,a)=\ell_D(w)-\ell_D(z)-1
\]
for the intrinsic edge \(z\to_Dw\) with ambient root \(a\). This follows by applying the plane inversion-change formula and replacing the two plane inversion counts by intrinsic lengths. It is the bridge from half-root counting to the exponent used in the local product comparison.

Localization and edge-image comparison use this factor to compare embedded edge images. Hilbert comparison uses its degree to normalize the comparison, and uses the path matrix to recognize the accumulated result as a target R-matrix.

The topic supplies the connections used later: order data can transport the edge schedule, and the schedule has both an algebraic path interpretation and controlled local correction factors.

## Inversion changes by root plane

The partner involution pairs the positive root lines exchanged by one reflection. The inversion-sign calculation gives contribution zero or two for each pair. Choosing the smaller slope records one root from each contributing pair, and grouping those roots by their planes defines the plane weights. Their sum measures the excess length of an edge beyond one.
