# Coefficient algebras and graded modules

A root-labelled graph uses polynomial coefficients. A sheaf assigns modules over that polynomial ring, and its construction repeatedly takes graded covers, kernels, images, and quotients. This topic develops these objects and the numerical invariants used to compare them.

Choose a basis of the extended root space \(V\), of dimension \(N\). Its symmetric algebra is \(A=\mathbb R[X_1,\ldots,X_N]\), with every variable in degree one. The degree-one vector \(\sum c_i e_i\) corresponds to the polynomial \(\sum c_iX_i\). The extended dimensions contribute variables just as the original root directions do.

## Components, generators, and quotients

A grading gives each vector a unique finite sum of homogeneous components. The component maps extract those summands. The positive ideal is generated by positive-degree scalars; for a polynomial algebra it consists of polynomials with zero constant term. Reduction modulo this ideal records the homogeneous generators that a minimal cover must lift.

A homogeneous submodule is closed under component maps. This makes its own grading and the quotient grading well defined, and it lets kernels and images participate in degreewise exact sequences. A homogeneous basis turns those abstract components into explicit coefficient conditions: a generator of degree \(d\) contributes the coefficient space \(A_{n-d}\) in degree \(n\).

Scalar quotients have a second role at an edge. Reducing a saturated submodule modulo a label gives an injective map into the reduced ambient module. A scalar that acts injectively on the targets of a linear constraint makes its kernel saturated. These facts connect the graded language to the later upper-image calculation.

## Polynomial presentations of root-label quotients

Let \(P\subseteq V\) be a linear subspace and \(I_P\) the ideal generated by its degree-one polynomials. The linear-quotient theorem constructs
\[
A/I_P\simeq\operatorname{Sym}(V/P).
\]
On generators, the map sends the class of \(v\) to the degree-one element associated with its class in \(V/P\). The reverse map sends that class back to \([\iota(v)]\); it is well defined because changing \(v\) by an element of \(P\) does not change the latter class.

Choosing a basis of \(V/P\) makes the right side a polynomial ring, hence a domain. Therefore \(I_P\) is prime. This supplies the prime ideals at which localization and edge-image comparison localizes. For a one-dimensional root line, it also identifies the edge coefficient ring \(A/(\alpha_e)\).

The inversion-count theorem supplies a different numerical input: the number of positive roots made negative by \(w^{-1}\) is \(\ell(w)\). Later this becomes the degree of a product of incoming labels, because each label has degree one.

## Modules over the coefficient algebra

An \(A\)-module permits addition and multiplication by polynomials. A free module \(A^r\) has a basis in which every vector has a unique expression with polynomial coefficients. Surjectivity and injectivity of module maps are meaningful just as in linear algebra, but a nonzero polynomial need not be invertible.

For example, multiplication by \(\alpha\) on \(\mathbb R[\alpha]\) is injective and not surjective: the constant \(1\) is not a polynomial multiple of \(\alpha\). The quotient \(A/(\alpha)\) is finite as an \(A\)-module, generated by one element, but is not free, since \(\alpha\) kills that generator.

A grading is a direct-sum decomposition \(M=\bigoplus_n M_n\) compatible with polynomial degree. A vector is homogeneous of degree \(n\) if it belongs to \(M_n\). For \(A=\mathbb R[\alpha]\), \(A_n=\mathbb R\alpha^n\) when \(n\ge0\), and \(A_n=0\) when \(n<0\).

A degree-zero map sends \(M_n\) into the corresponding target piece. The shift convention is
\[
M(d)_n=M_{n-d}.
\]
Thus the generator \(1\) of \(A\), originally in degree zero, has degree \(d\) in \(A(d)\).

## Calculate a character and a Hilbert series

Suppose \(M\) is finite graded free with homogeneous basis degrees \(d_1,\ldots,d_r\). Its graded-rank polynomial is
\[
\operatorname{ch}(M;q)=\sum_{i=1}^r q^{d_i}.
\]
The coefficient of a power of q counts the homogeneous basis generators in that degree.

The Hilbert series records the latter:
\[
H_M(q)=\sum_n\dim_{\mathbb R}(M_n)q^n.
\]
For \(A=\mathbb R[\alpha]\), there is one monomial in every nonnegative degree, so
\[
H_A(q)=1+q+q^2+\cdots=\frac1{1-q}\quad(0<q<1).
\]
For \(M=A\oplus A(2)\), the dimensions are \(1,1,2,2,\ldots\), hence
\[
\operatorname{ch}(M;q)=1+q^2,\qquad
H_M(q)=\frac{1+q^2}{1-q}.
\]
With \(N\) polynomial variables, the denominator is \((1-q)^N\). Each basis generator contributes its shift times the series of the coefficient ring.

A nonfree module can still have a Hilbert series. For \(A/(\alpha)\), only degree zero remains, so its series is \(1\). This is why the later proof can use Hilbert values before it has proved all the needed freeness statements.

## Minimal covers and the role of projectivity

Let \(J=(X_1,\ldots,X_N)\). Passing to \(M/JM\) removes all positive-degree scalar multiples and leaves a finite-dimensional real vector space. Choose a homogeneous basis of this quotient and lift its vectors to \(M\). These lifts define a map from a finite direct sum of shifted copies of \(A\) onto \(M\).

Graded Nakayama proves that this map is surjective. To see the mechanism, subtract a combination of the selected lifts from a homogeneous vector. The remaining vector lies in \(JM\), so it is expressed using positive-degree coefficients multiplying vectors of smaller degree. Repeat in those smaller degrees. The grading is bounded below, so the descent ends. The resulting map is a minimal graded free cover.

Projectivity is the splitting property: a surjection onto a projective module has a linear right inverse. If the target \(M\) above is projective, the free cover \(F\to M\) splits, with kernel \(K\). Minimality gives \(K\subseteq JF\); applying the projection onto \(K\) gives \(K=JK\).

The source's projective-to-free proof then uses a determinant argument. Finite generation writes generators of \(K\) as a matrix of \(J\)-coefficients times themselves. The determinant of the identity minus this matrix annihilates \(K\), but has constant term one and is therefore nonzero. Since \(K\) lies inside a free module over a domain, multiplication by that determinant is injective. Thus \(K=0\). The chosen lifts are a homogeneous free basis.

This explains why finite generation alone is insufficient, and why the later reverse argument first proves projectivity.

## Exactness can be checked degree by degree

For a degree-zero surjection \(M\to N\) with kernel \(K\), a homogeneous vector in \(N_n\) has a homogeneous preimage in \(M_n\): take any preimage and retain its degree-\(n\) component. Consequently
\[
0\longrightarrow K_n\longrightarrow M_n\longrightarrow N_n\longrightarrow0
\]
is exact, and dimensions add.

For the quotient \(A\to A/(\alpha)\), its kernel is \(\alpha A\), whose basis generator has degree one. We obtain
\[
\frac1{1-q}=\frac q{1-q}+1.
\]
Hilbert comparison uses this identity for partial kernels and edge images. The reverse traversal later proves the partial kernels free.

The tools to carry forward are: linear labels and prime quotients; homogeneous maps and shifts; minimal free covers; projective-to-free conversion; and Hilbert additivity with its convergence hypotheses.
