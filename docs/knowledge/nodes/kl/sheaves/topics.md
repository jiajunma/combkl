# Moment-graph sheaves, support, and duality

This topic constructs modules on a labelled Bruhat interval, studies their compatible sections, and uses support and duality to relate stalks to outgoing kernels. Its inputs are the ordered interval, polynomial edge labels from the extended realization, and finite graded modules. Its outputs are boundary sheaves, stalk–costalk duality, and the actual-character reciprocity used in the invariance proof.

The two-vertex interval \(1<s\) and \(A=\mathbb R[\alpha]\) provide a running example. The same stalks, restrictions, and sections will carry us from the first definitions to an explicit duality map.

A graph sheaf is an explicit collection of modules and linear maps on a directed graph. Its section equations compare values at adjacent vertices after applying the two edge restrictions.

## Modules at vertices and edges

Write the vertices as \(x<y\), and let \(e:x\to y\) be the single edge. Define
\[
B^x=A,\qquad B^y=A,\qquad B^e=A/(\alpha).
\]
The symbols \(B^x,B^y\) are the vertex modules, also called stalks. The symbol \(B^e\) is the edge module. There are two restriction maps
\[
\rho_{x,e}:B^x\to B^e,\qquad \rho_{y,e}:B^y\to B^e.
\]
In this example both are the quotient map \(f\mapsto f\bmod\alpha\).

The direction \(x\to y\) specifies which endpoint is lower and which is upper. Both restrictions go into the common edge module, where the two endpoint values can be compared.

On a general graph the definition supplies this data for every vertex and edge. A graded sheaf also requires compatible gradings and degree-zero restriction maps.

## Section equations and a homogeneous basis

An assignment chooses one stalk vector at every vertex. For the example it is a pair \((f,g)\in A^2\). It is a compatible section when the two values agree after restriction:
\[
f\bmod\alpha=g\bmod\alpha,
\]
or equivalently \(f-g\in\alpha A\).

Thus the global section module is
\[
\Gamma(B)=\{(f,g)\in A^2:f-g\in\alpha A\}.
\]
Write \(f-g=\alpha h\). Then
\[
(f,g)=g(1,1)+h(\alpha,0).
\]
The expression is unique: the second coordinate determines \(g\), and injectivity of multiplication by \(\alpha\) determines \(h\). Therefore \(\Gamma(B)\) is free with homogeneous basis degrees zero and one.

Each stalk is free of rank one. The global section module has rank two, with basis degrees zero and one. The degree-one generator records the freedom to change the lower value by a multiple of the edge label.

## Global generation and flabbiness

Global generation asks whether every stalk vector can be realized as the value of a global section. In the example, a prescribed polynomial \(f\) at either vertex is realized by \((f,f)\), so both stalks are generated by global sections.

An upper set is a vertex subset that contains every vertex above any of its members. The nontrivial proper upper set in this example is \(\{y\}\). Flabbiness asks whether every compatible section on an upper set extends to a global section.

A polynomial \(g\) prescribed at \(y\) extends to \((g,g)\), so the example is flabby. On a graph with several vertices, prescribing compatible values on an entire upper set imposes more conditions than prescribing a value at one stalk. The two extension statements become separate inputs to the boundary-image theorem.

Relative flabbiness is the extension statement for sets closed under the edges that remain relevant after localization. It will be used in localization and edge-image comparison when some edge modules become zero.

## The boundary image as the target of the new stalk

Suppose only the portion of a sheaf strictly above \(x\) has been constructed. Restrict a section there to each edge leaving \(x\). The resulting tuple lies in the product of the outgoing edge modules. The set of all such tuples is the boundary image at \(x\).

For the one-edge example, the upper region has the stalk \(A\) at \(y\), and the boundary map is
\[
A\longrightarrow A/(\alpha).
\]
Its image is all of \(A/(\alpha)\). To attach a stalk at \(x\), we need a free module surjecting onto this image. The minimal graded free cover is the quotient map \(A\to A/(\alpha)\), giving exactly the stalk and restriction we started with.

For several outgoing edges, the boundary image need not be the full product: values arriving from the upper region must already satisfy its internal section equations. Taking a minimal cover of this image retains those constraints. The theorem [[node:kl.sheaves.boundary_image_identity]] identifies it with the image of the lower stalk map: global generation gives one containment, and extension from the strict upper set gives the other.

Start at the top vertex \(b\), where the stalk is normalized to \(A\), and repeat the construction downward. Finiteness of the vertex set guarantees termination. The boundary-existence theorem verifies all required sheaf properties. The uniqueness theorem compares two such constructions by identifying their upper boundary images and extending the resulting isomorphism through the minimal covers.

## Kernels remember which outgoing conditions have been imposed

For a set \(F\) of outgoing edges at \(x\), define
\[
K_x(F)=\{m\in B^x:\rho_{x,e}(m)=0\text{ for every }e\in F\}.
\]
No conditions gives \(K_x(\varnothing)=B^x\). Imposing all outgoing conditions gives the full outgoing kernel, called the costalk in the later comparison.

In the one-edge example,
\[
K_x(\varnothing)=A,\qquad K_x(\{e\})=\alpha A,\qquad K_y(\varnothing)=A.
\]
The last equality holds because the top vertex has no outgoing edges. Removing the condition \(e\) enlarges the lower kernel from \(\alpha A\) to \(A\).

On a larger graph there are intermediate kernels corresponding to different subsets of conditions. The simultaneous induction establishes freeness for the suffix kernels used in the comparison. At each step, an exact sequence measures a change of kernel by its image in one edge module.

## Commuting restriction maps in a sheaf isomorphism

A sheaf isomorphism consists of an isomorphism at every vertex and edge, commuting with both restrictions. For example, if \(j_x\) and \(j_e\) are the lower stalk and edge maps, they must satisfy
\[
j_e\rho^B_{x,e}=\rho^C_{x,e}j_x.
\]
This equation ensures that a kernel or image in \(B\) goes to the corresponding kernel or image in \(C\).

In the free module \(A\), the submodules \(A\) and \(\alpha A\) both have rank one, and their inclusions have different images. A commuting sheaf isomorphism tracks those inclusions. Later comparisons use it to place the relevant edge images in a common module.

## Splitting sheaves and decomposing their kernels

A split inclusion consists of sheaf maps \(i:B\to C\) and \(p:C\to B\) with \(pi=1_B\). For \(C=B\oplus D\), the familiar maps are inclusion into the first component and projection back to it. Their composite \(ip\) is a projector on \(C\).

Under the top-splitting theorem’s hypotheses, a split map at the normalized top extends to a split pair of sheaf maps. Primitivity then controls when a nonzero summand already exhausts the whole sheaf.

Applying these results repeatedly yields a decomposition into shifted boundary models. A model \(O_y\) is normalized at \(y\) and vanishes outside the lower ideal below \(y\). Schematically,
\[
C\simeq\bigoplus_{y,j}O_y(d_{y,j}).
\]
This is one sheaf isomorphism. At a stalk it gives a direct sum of stalks; on an outgoing kernel it gives the direct sum of the corresponding kernels.

For example, \(O_b\oplus O_b(2)\) has stalk character \((1+q^2)\operatorname{ch}(O_b^x)\) and kernel character \((1+q^2)\operatorname{ch}(K_x^{O_b})\). The same factor occurs because the same summands define both modules. The character argument uses this shared decomposition to cancel lower models.

The objects to retain are therefore explicit: stalks hold vectors, edge modules impose congruences, sections solve the congruences, boundary images determine new stalk covers, and outgoing kernels record vanishing conditions.


## From coordinate images to a sheaf

The pair-projection sheaf uses these stalk images. On an edge it remembers whether the two endpoint values come from one common word element. The simultaneous free-stalk/edge-kernel theorem proves that the stalks are free and that the upper restriction kernel is the edge label times the upper stalk.

A global word element gives compatible values on every edge. To recover a common word element from compatible coordinates, the flabbiness proof establishes a lifting theorem on upper sets through projective-range and denominator arguments.

The word module supplies the coordinate values, the word sheaf records their pairwise congruences, and the stalk and flabbiness theorems supply the hypotheses for decomposition and duality.

## Supported sections and incoming label factors

A section supported at \(x\) has every other coordinate zero. Its value at \(x\) must therefore vanish under both the incoming upper restrictions and the outgoing lower restrictions. The full outgoing kernel already supplies the second group of equations.

For each incoming edge, the upper-quotient condition identifies its kernel with the multiples of that edge label. Let
\[
\beta_x=\prod_{e:\,t(e)=x}\alpha_e.
\]
The theorem [[node:kl.sheaves.costalk_support]] proves that multiplication by \(\beta_x\), followed by zero extension, gives
\[
K_x\xrightarrow{\sim}\Gamma(B)_{\{x\}},
\qquad k\longmapsto\delta_x(\beta_x k).
\]
The proof uses the prime linear labels twice. Distinct incoming primes force a supported stalk value to be divisible by their product. That product remains nonzero modulo every outgoing label, so it can be cancelled in each outgoing edge module.

In the running example, \(\beta_x=1\) and \(\beta_y=\alpha\). Thus the lower kernel \(\alpha A\) gives the sections \((\alpha h,0)\), while the upper kernel \(A\) gives \((0,\alpha h)\) after multiplication by \(\alpha\).

## Integral cuts and the stalk dual

Let \(p\) be the product of all edge labels. It annihilates every edge module. Consequently
\[
C_xm=p\,\delta_x(m_x)
\]
is a global section for every global section \(m\). This is the integral vertex cut.

A perfect section pairing identifies \(\Gamma(B)\) with its \(A\)-linear dual. Its two support-orthogonality equations imply that the cuts are balanced:
\[
\langle C_xm,n\rangle=\langle m,C_xn\rangle.
\]
The proof compares both expressions, multiplied by \(p\), with \(\langle C_xm,C_xn\rangle\), and cancels \(p\).

This balance equation identifies supported sections with the functionals that vanish on the evaluation kernel \(\ker\pi_x\). Global generation makes \(\pi_x:\Gamma(B)\to B^x\) surjective. A supported section therefore determines a stalk functional by
\[
\Phi_x(m)(v)=\langle m,n\rangle\quad\text{for any section }n\text{ with }n_x=v.
\]
Two choices of \(n\) differ by an element of \(\ker\pi_x\), on which the pairing is zero. Conversely, a stalk functional \(\lambda\) is represented by \(D^{-1}(\lambda\circ\pi_x)\), which the support-annihilator theorem proves is supported at \(x\).

The two isomorphisms compose:
\[
K_x\xrightarrow{\ k\mapsto\delta_x(\beta_x k)\ }
\Gamma(B)_{\{x\}}
\xrightarrow{\ \Phi_x\ }(B^x)^*.
\]
This is the costalk-duality map. It transfers a finite dual basis to the full outgoing kernel, proving that kernel free. The homogeneity equation of the pairing then determines the basis degrees.

## Degree reversal under support duality

For a homogeneous basis vector \(m\) of degree \(d\), its coordinate functional has degree \(-d\): on a degree-\(j\) vector, it extracts a coefficient of degree \(j-d\).

The homogeneous-representative theorem proves that a degree-\(r\) functional on sections is represented through a shift-\(L\) perfect pairing by a section of degree \(r+L\). The proof takes homogeneous components and uses injectivity of the pairing isomorphism. Removing a nonzero incoming product of degree \(n_x\) then produces a kernel vector of degree \(r+L-n_x\), by injectivity of scalar multiplication. Thus every degree in the following calculation is justified by a component argument.

The constructed perfect pairing on a reduced word of length \(m\) sends input degrees \(i,j\) to polynomial degree \(i+j-m\). Support duality then identifies the full outgoing kernel at a vertex \(x\) with a shifted stalk dual. The product of incoming labels accounts for \(\ell(x)\) degrees. Thus a stalk basis degree \(d_i\) becomes a kernel basis degree
\[
m-\ell(x)-d_i.
\]
Since \(m=\ell(b)\) for a reduced word with top \(b\), its character becomes
\[
K_x^{\mathbf s}(q)=q^{\ell(b)-\ell(x)}C_x^{\mathbf s}(q^{-1}).
\]

In our one-edge example, the lower stalk is \(A\), with basis degree zero, and its outgoing kernel is \(\alpha A\), with basis degree one. The formula predicts \(q\cdot1=q\), exactly as calculated directly.

We can also see the duality map itself in this example. Tensor induction gives
\[
\langle a\otimes b,c\otimes d\rangle=ac\,\operatorname{tr}(bd).
\]
In the basis \(1\otimes1,1\otimes\alpha\), the pairing matrix is again
\(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), now over \(A\).
Write two compatible section pairs as
\((f,g)=(u+\alpha v,u-\alpha v)\) and
\((f',g')=(u'+\alpha v',u'-\alpha v')\). Their pairing is
\[
uv'+vu'=\frac{ff'-gg'}{2\alpha}.
\]
The displayed quotient belongs to \(A\): both pairs agree modulo \(\alpha\), so the numerator is divisible by \(\alpha\).

A section supported only at the lower vertex has the form \((\alpha h,0)\). The stalk functional \(f\mapsto cf\) is represented by the supported section \((2\alpha c,0)\), because
\[
\langle(2\alpha c,0),(f,g)\rangle=cf.
\]
Thus the degree-zero dual generator of the stalk corresponds to a supported generator of degree one. The factor \(2\) is an invertible scalar. This calculation shows concretely how the outgoing kernel is related to a shifted stalk dual; the general support-duality theorem constructs this relation with all incoming labels accounted for.

## Adjoint maps, image sheaves, and the boundary pairing

The splitting theorem supplies maps \(i:B\to W\) and \(p:W\to B\), with \(pi=1\), from the normalized boundary sheaf into a reduced-word sheaf. Their composite \(e=ip\) is a projector. The section equivalence identifies its action on global sections with an endomorphism \(F\) of the word module.

The perfect word pairing defines
\[
F^*=D^{-1}F^\vee D,\qquad
\langle F^*m,n\rangle=\langle m,Fn\rangle.
\]
Composition reverses under the adjoint, so \(F^*\) is also a projector. Homogeneous representatives show that it has degree zero.

To descend this operator to stalks, fix a projection \(\pi_x\). Its kernel consists exactly of the word elements annihilated by every supported element at \(x\). The original sheaf map preserves that supported submodule. Hence, if \(\pi_x(m)=0\),
\[
\langle s,F^*m\rangle=\langle Fs,m\rangle=0
\]
for every supported \(s\), proving \(\pi_x(F^*m)=0\). Quotient descent defines the adjoint stalk map. The pair-projection quotient supplies the edge map and both commuting squares. This produces the graded sheaf projector \(e^*\).

Its image sheaf \(C\) has the component images as stalks and edge modules. A section of this image sheaf is fixed by the section projector, and every value of the section projector is an image-sheaf section. Thus
\[
\Gamma(C)\simeq\operatorname{im}F^*.
\]
The algebraic adjoint-summand theorem now gives
\[
\Gamma(C)\simeq\Gamma(B)^*,\qquad
c\longmapsto(w\mapsto\langle c,i(w)\rangle).
\]
Its inverse represents the extended functional \(\lambda\circ p\) by the perfect word form.

Primitivity and top splitting recognize \(C\) as another copy of \(B\). The next step proves that the specified comparison
\[
h:B\to C,\qquad h(v)=e^*i(v)
\]
is an isomorphism. This comparison is bijective at the top; the boundary minimal-cover argument extends bijectivity down through the interval.

Pull the displayed duality back through \(h\). For global sections \(v,w\) of \(B\),
\[
\langle h(v),i(w)\rangle
=\langle F^*i(v),i(w)\rangle
=\langle i(v),Fi(w)\rangle
=\langle i(v),i(w)\rangle.
\]
The last equality uses \(Fi=i\). This proves that the induced perfect boundary pairing is the word form on the included sections. Its symmetry, degree shift, and support conditions pass through the inclusion maps.

## Cancellation of lower boundary models

Let the word sheaf decompose into shifted boundary models with top vertices \(y\le b\). Write \(m_y(q)\) for the polynomial counting its shifts at center \(y\). One sheaf decomposition gives both identities
\[
C_z^{\mathbf s}=\sum_{y\le b}m_yC_{z,y},\qquad
K_x^{\mathbf s}=\sum_{y\le b}m_yK_{x,y}.
\]
Use zero for \(C_{z,y}\) when \(z\not\le y\).

The word-character formula and Hecke bar identity separately prove
\[
K_x^{\mathbf s}=\sum_{z\le b}R_{x,z}C_z^{\mathbf s}.
\]
Substitute the two decompositions and interchange the finite sums:
\[
\sum_{y\le b}m_y\left(K_{x,y}-\sum_{z\le b}R_{x,z}C_{z,y}\right)=0.
\]
For every \(y<b\), the bracket vanishes by induction on the top length. At \(y=b\), the multiplicity is one: only the top model contributes at \(b\), and the word's top stalk has character one. The equation therefore forces the remaining bracket to vanish.

Replacing \(K_{x,b}\) by its duality expression gives
\[
q^{\ell(b)-\ell(x)}C_{x,b}(q^{-1})
=\sum_{x\le z\le b}R_{x,z}(q)C_{z,b}(q).
\]
This is the identity used later. For \([1,s]\), it reads \(q=1+(q-1)\), so the example checks all three terms.


## Local intersection and character identification

The boundary pairing also defines a local intersection map from the dual of a stalk into that stalk. Its image is the full outgoing kernel. The local degree theorem applies to a specified nonzero diagonal coefficient.

The actual boundary character \(C\) satisfies the reciprocal identity developed here. The canonical family \(P\) is characterized by reciprocity together with its strict degree bound. Their identification is the separate condition in [[node:kl.duality.character_identity_condition]]. The invariance argument uses the proved identity for \(C\) to establish R-invariance, then applies the canonical characterization to \(P\).
