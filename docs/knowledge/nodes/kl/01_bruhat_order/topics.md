# 1. Coxeter systems and Bruhat order

This chapter identifies what can be read from the interval order and what must still be reconstructed later. Starting from a Coxeter system and its length function, it defines upward reflection edges and strong Bruhat order, then derives the finite combinatorics needed throughout the proof.

The principal outputs are the subword criterion, finiteness of lower ideals, preservation of rank differences by interval isomorphisms, and finite generator support below a fixed element. These results make later sums and inductions finite even when the ambient Coxeter group is infinite. No realization or sheaf is needed here.

## Reflections, length, and intervals

An upward reflection edge has the form \(x\to tx\), where \(t\) is a reflection and \(\ell(x)<\ell(tx)\). Its length jump need not be one. Bruhat order is generated by chains of these edges, whereas a cover means that no element lies strictly between two endpoints in the order.

An interval isomorphism initially preserves the order and therefore the covers. Preservation of every reflection edge is a later theorem in chapter 6. Keeping these notions separate explains why unlabelled graph reconstruction is a substantive step of the proof.

The rank difference \(d(x,y)=\ell(y)-\ell(x)\) measures the size of the induction problem. The rank-preservation theorem below shows that an order isomorphism preserves this difference, even though the absolute lengths of the endpoints in two Coxeter systems may differ.

## Exchange and finiteness

Strong exchange explains how shortening multiplication by a reflection deletes a letter from a reduced word. It has two uses. First, transporting reflection edges by a simple reflection gives monotonicity of the lower and upper simple envelopes, hence the lifting property. Second, repeated deletion leads to the reduced-subword criterion.

For a fixed reduced word for \(b\), every element below \(b\) is represented by one of its subwords. There are finitely many subwords, so the lower ideal and each interval inside it are finite. The same description confines every such element to the simple generators appearing in that word. The finite-support theorem retains that stronger conclusion, which is needed when the generating set of the original system is infinite.

The rank-preservation result uses the ranked structure of Bruhat intervals to identify length differences from the order. It is read after the subword and interval results because the later invariance argument must restrict isomorphisms to smaller intervals and compare their ranks. ## How later chapters use these results

Chapter 2 uses finite intervals to define convolution sums and recursive polynomial families. Chapter 5 uses simple-reflection lifting in the induction on word sheaves. Chapter 6 starts from the cover relations and reconstructs the remaining reflection edges. Chapter 9 uses rank preservation to justify each smaller induction instance, and finite generator support to construct the parabolic models in [[node:kl.main.finite_model_transport]].

The chain to retain is: exchange controls words; words control the lower ideal; the lower ideal supplies finite ranked data for the later algebra and geometry.
