# The positive ideal is the kernel of degree-zero projection

For a nonnegatively graded commutative algebra over a field \(k\), degree-zero projection is a ring homomorphism \(\epsilon:A\to A\) with
\[
\ker\epsilon=A_+.
\]
For the polynomial grading, the degree-zero piece consists of constants and every polynomial is congruent to a scalar modulo \(A_+\).

*Proof.*

### Step 1. Check the additive and unit laws

Component projection is \(k\)-linear, so it preserves zero and addition. The algebra unit lies in \(A_0\), hence \(\pi_0(1)=1\).

### Step 2. Check multiplication on homogeneous components

Take \(a\in A_i\), \(b\in A_j\). Negative-degree pieces are zero, so a nonzero contribution has \(i,j\ge0\).

If \(i+j=0\), then \(i=j=0\). Both projections fix the factors and their product, giving
\(\pi_0(ab)=ab=\pi_0(a)\pi_0(b)\).

If \(i+j>0\), the product has zero degree-zero component. At least one of \(i,j\) is positive, so at least one factor has zero projection. Both sides are zero.

Every element has a finite homogeneous decomposition. Distributing products over those finite sums extends the identity to arbitrary \(a,b\), proving multiplicativity.

### Step 3. Include the positive ideal in the kernel

A positive homogeneous generator has zero degree-zero component. Since the kernel of a ring homomorphism is an ideal, it contains the ideal generated by these elements. Thus \(A_+\subseteq\ker\epsilon\).

### Step 4. Recover a kernel element from positive components

If \(\pi_0(a)=0\), decompose \(a\) into its finite sum of homogeneous components. Its negative components are zero by nonnegativity; its degree-zero component is zero by the hypothesis.

Every remaining component has positive degree and is itself a generator of \(A_+\). Their finite sum belongs to \(A_+\). This proves the reverse containment.

### Step 5. Specialize to polynomial coefficients

A monomial in variables of degree one has total degree equal to the sum of its nonnegative exponents. Negative total degree is impossible. Total degree zero forces every exponent to be zero, so the monomial is one.

Consequently a degree-zero polynomial is constant. Subtracting a polynomial's constant term leaves only positive-degree monomials, so the difference belongs to \(A_+\).
